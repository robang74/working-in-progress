## Comparing "The Tragedy of Complexity": Oehmke & Zawadowski vs. Foglietta

Based on the provided PDF documents, here is a comparison between the economic research paper *"The Tragedy of Complexity"* by Martin Oehmke (LSE) and Adam Zawadowski (CEU) and the article by Roberto A. Foglietta titled *"The Tragedy of Complexity"*.

> **Note on versions.** The uploaded paper PDF is the working-paper version dated September 6, 2019. The paper was subsequently published in *Management Science* (INFORMS), published online June 1, 2026, doi: 10.1287/mnsc.2024.08215. The published version uses "suppliers" (instead of "producers") and "depth of understanding" `d_i = t_i/c_i` (instead of "effective attention"), and extends the applications to financial institutions facing regulatory bodies and division heads communicating with a CEO — settings in which "suppliers of messages compete for the attention of a single party," which strengthens the thematic bridge to Foglietta's concerns about public discourse. The comparison below refers to the uploaded 2019 version.

---

### 1. Are they about the same subject?

**Yes and no.** They share the same title and a broad thematic inspiration — the idea that the way modern agents deal with complexity produces systemic "tragic" outcomes that no one intends but everyone generates — but they approach the subject from entirely different angles:

* **The Oehmke & Zawadowski paper** is a **formal equilibrium economic theory** of product (or message) complexity. It models a consumer with a limited attention/time budget, producers who choose complexity and extract a share of surplus, and the resulting market inefficiency.

* **Foglietta's article** is a **sociopolitical and geopolitical commentary** (transcribed from LinkedIn posts, 1st and 2nd editions, October 2026) that uses the phrase as a lens to critique public discourse: polarized "either/or" debates, media simplification, and geopolitical decision-making (the Eurozone, Australian renewable energy, the Siberian plague incident, the Trump–Russia diesel deal).

---

### 2. Overlapping concepts

Despite the difference in methodology (rigorous mathematics vs. socio-political commentary), several conceptual threads genuinely overlap:

* **Complexity as something that is *strategically shaped*, not merely suffered.** In the paper, complexity is a choice variable: producers distort it to capture attention, and the distortion is systematic, not accidental. In Foglietta, simplification (or complication) is likewise a strategic move — by media, politicians, and commentators — not a simple failure of intelligence.

* **The dumbing down of complex public issues.** The paper itself applies the model to media outlets and politicians oversimplifying complex policy questions (its example is the Brexit debate being reduced to the UK's budget contribution). This is precisely Foglietta's core territory: the "skipping of the versus," where debate collapses into "stadium fanaticism" and the method hypothesis → thesis → confutation → correction is not even contemplated. Foglietta's claim that "we are losing awareness of the method required to understand" complex problems mirrors the paper's "giving up" region, where the consumer abandons any attempt to understand very complex goods.

* **Scarce attention / bounded processing capacity.** Both build on the premise that human attention and cognitive capacity are limited resources. The paper makes this literal (a time budget `T` allocated across `N` goods); Foglietta makes it epistemic (understanding "isn't as cheap as having an opinion").

* **Counterintuitive comparative statics.** The paper shows that more attention capacity can make excessive complexity *more* likely (the complexity paradox), and that more competing goods produce inefficient simplification (the curse of variety). Foglietta advances the analogous counterintuition that more sophisticated arguments do not necessarily help: their sophistication is "precisely what prevents them from being received." In both cases, adding resources or information does not fix the distortion, because the distortion is generated by incentives.

* **The "tragedy" framing.** In both, the tragedy is not complexity itself but a systemic incentive structure — market competition for attention in the paper, social rewards of allegiance over inquiry in the article — that corrupts how complexity is handled.

One parallel should be stated with care: Foglietta's critique of institutionalized fallacies (e.g., the 3%-of-GDP debt rule generalized from a single French village's experience) concerns *reasoning errors* (overgeneralization, coincidence vs. causation). The paper's mechanism is not a reasoning bias but an **equilibrium externality**: even fully rational agents produce inefficiency because each ignores the shadow cost their attention grabbing imposes on others. The common lesson is narrower and sounder: complex systems resist one-size-fits-all shortcuts, whether the shortcut is a fallacious rule of thumb or an attention-grabbing product design.

### 3. Key differences

| Feature | Oehmke & Zawadowski | Foglietta |
|---|---|---|
| **Nature of work** | Formal academic research paper: model setup, propositions, proofs, comparative statics | Opinion essay / social-media transcription aimed at divulgation of current socio-political events |
| **Primary domain** | Microeconomics: product design, financial markets, consumer attention allocation, regulation, intra-firm communication | Geopolitics (EU monetary policy, Russia/Ukraine, US trade) and societal epistemics (media narratives, binary polarization) |
| **Definition of "complexity"** | A value-enhancing attribute of a good that requires consumer time/attention to unlock its value (`v_i(c_i, t_i/c_i)`) | The hidden, multi-layered, interconnected nature of real-world systems — "the submerged part of the iceberg" that public discourse ignores |
| **The "tragedy" (mechanism)** | An **attention externality**: producers non-cooperatively distort complexity to divert scarce consumer attention from competing goods, so equilibrium complexity departs from the social planner's optimum in either direction (too complex or too simple) | An **epistemic failure**: the erosion of the collective ability and willingness to engage in structured inquiry, replaced by identity-first allegiance and oversimplification |
| **Diagnostic tool** | Sign of the attention-grabbing effect `∂t_i/∂c_i`; the "software update effect" (value falls at fixed attention ⇒ excessive complexity); goods well understood (high `t_i/c_i`) are too complex, poorly understood goods too simple | Absence of the hypothesis → thesis → confutation → correction sequence; the skipped "versus"; the substitution of belonging for inquiry |
| **Benchmark** | A benevolent social planner maximizing total surplus | An ideal of rational public deliberation (Marcus Aurelius: "The opinion of 10,000 people is of no value if none of them know anything about the subject") |
| **Prescription** | Regulation that induces producers to internalize the shadow cost of attention (the paper itself notes that a simple "reduce complexity" mandate is wrong, since complexity can also be too low) | Restore awareness of the method of inquiry and reintroduce the adversarial "versus" into public debate |
| **Evidence** | Derivations, proofs, closed-form comparative statics | Anecdotes, current events, historical analogies (Vajont, the 3% rule) |

---

### 4. Summary

The paper and the article do not address the same primary subject:

* The Oehmke & Zawadowski paper is a **microeconomic equilibrium theory** of complexity choice, consumer attention, and the inefficiencies generated by the attention externality.

* Foglietta's work is an **informal socio-epistemological commentary** on the collapse of public debate and the institutionalization of simplification.

The overlap is real but limited: both treat simplification/overcomplication as a *strategic distortion* rather than an accident, both center on scarce human attention, both note that adding capacity or sophistication does not cure the distortion, and both reserve the word "tragedy" for systemic outcomes that individual optimization produces against everyone's interest. Notably, the paper's own media-and-politics application (and, in the published *Management Science* version, its treatment of competing "suppliers of messages") supplies a microeconomic mechanism that can underwrite — at least partially — the societal information failures Foglietta describes. The two texts are therefore best read as complementary: one explains *why* rational competing agents distort complexity, the other laments *that* whole societies now skip the debate that complexity demands.
