Looking at the plan in your attachment — $20B up‑front deposit + a 10‑year annuity (~$89B nominal, i.e. half of obligated) from a third party that assumes all future US obligations, with the US exiting cleanly — here's my assessment from the US point of view. The short version: **the parts that are legally possible are already happening in miniature (PURL), the parts that would make the numbers work mostly can't exist, and the politics are surprisingly favorable.**

## 1. The core accounting problem: there's almost nothing to "sell"

The plan assumes the US can transfer a $178B "book" and recover 60–70% of it. But as your own attachment establishes, roughly $165B+ of the obligated amount is **grant‑based spending that has already flowed to US defense contractors, Ukraine's budget, and US stockpile replenishment**. Money already spent is not a receivable — there is no legal claim on Ukraine or anyone else attached to it, so there is no asset to transfer, swap, or securitize. The negative IRR (−7% to −9%) computed in the attachment is therefore somewhat artificial: you can't lose return on money you never had a right to recover. From the US **budget** perspective, converting even part of future grant commitments into $109B of cash inflows would be found money, not a haircut.

What *is* transferable is only the **unobligated/undisbursed tail** — roughly $17B not yet obligated and ~$62B obligated‑but‑not‑yet‑disbursed (contracted deliveries, future tranches). That's the real dealable envelope, not $178B.

## 2. The third party's economics don't close

Run the plan from the third party's side: they deposit $20B, assume future cash‑outs that could reach tens of billions, and receive back only $89B nominal over 10 years — with Ukraine (public debt already >100% of GDP) as the nominal repayer. The third party's IRR is deeply negative unless the "repayment" is actually underwritten by someone else (frozen Russian assets, the EU budget). No rational actor takes that deal voluntarily; only a **strategically motivated payer** — i.e., Europe buying US disengagement — would, and even then it would be framed as burden‑sharing, not a purchase.

Notably, Europe's demonstrated appetite for this is small: PURL pledges announced in February 2026 totaled only about **$584 million** — three orders of magnitude below what your plan requires. And PURL already shows the catch: the US is criticized for taking a "10% markup" while getting credit for aid others paid for, meaning Washington monetizing the exit is politically toxic to the very allies you'd need as buyers.

## 3. What already works: PURL/NSATU as the feasible mini‑version

Your plan's realistic kernel already exists: through NSATU and PURL (launched July 2025), NATO allies fund US‑origin weapons for Ukraine while the US gets paid, keeps industrial activity, and retains influence. Feasible scaling of your idea = **expanding PURL‑style funding of undisbursed US contracts plus a European‑backed annuity**, not a sale of the whole $178B book.

## 4. Welcome-ness from the US point of view

**Strongly welcome in the White House.** The plan is a perfect fit for the administration's transactional framing: Trump has repeatedly claimed the US should recoup its Ukraine spending (citing figures like $300B), and the April 2025 minerals deal already codifies the logic of treating aid as an investment to be paid back. A deal that brings in $20B now, ~$9–10B/year for a decade, zero future outlays, and a clean exit would be sold domestically as a triumph — the negative IRR framing would never survive contact with a press release. Burden‑shifting to Europe also polls well and matches the "America First" base.

**Mixed to hostile in Congress and the foreign‑policy establishment.** Bipartisan congressional majorities built the Ukraine appropriations; unilaterally converting grants into a paid exit would require new transfer/repurposing authority and could face Impoundment Control Act and Article I spending‑power fights. Hawks in both parties would read "US exit" as abandoning deterrence; the Afghanistan analogy you invoke is precisely the optic Washington wants to avoid. Even CEPA‑style centrist analysis frames the model as needing *more* US leadership, not less.

**Precedent risk cuts both ways.** If the US sells its exposure once, every ally (and adversary) recalibrates US commitments as rentable and exit‑prone — expensive for credibility. But proponents would counter that the minerals deal already crossed that Rubicon.

Run their break‑even: they put in $20B deposit + assume $79B of future cash‑outs = $99B at risk. At a modest 4% hurdle rate over 10 years they need:

- **$12.21B/year** to break even — versus the ~$4–4.6B/year on the table.

## Kimi 

You've just described — almost exactly — the deal that was actually signed on April 30, 2025. That's both the strongest validation of your reasoning and the clearest evidence of where its limits are. Let me separate what the US can get, what it's worth in PV terms, and why the hard version of your list (ownership, veto, trade privileges) was deliberately left out.

## 1. This already exists — the Reconstruction Investment Fund

The US–Ukraine "minerals deal" *is* a strategic-concession-for-aid arrangement, not a debt swap:

- Ukraine contributes **50% of royalties, rents and license fees from new resource projects** (critical minerals, oil, gas); existing state projects (Naftogaz, Ukrnafta) are explicitly exempt
- **US military assistance counts as a capital contribution** to the fund — precisely the "aid → equity" conversion your structure implies
- **50/50 governance**, DFC as US partner, profits **reinvested in Ukraine for the first 10 years**
- Went fully operational December 2025, first investments expected during 2026, with just **$150M committed by each side** so far

Note how far this is from your full list: **no land ownership, no veto over reconstruction, no trade privileges, no security guarantee**. Every one of those was negotiated away or never asked, because they are the infeasible parts.

## 2. What the concessions are actually worth (the valuation reality)

This is where the plan's arithmetic meets geology:

- The "$15 trillion resources" figures circulate in political rhetoric, but **~40% of critical minerals and most rare earth deposits are in Russian-occupied or contested territory** — including Europe's largest hard-rock lithium deposit (Shevchenko, Donetsk) and the Azovske/Mazurivske rare earth sites
- **Zero cash for a decade**: profits are locked into Ukrainian reinvestment for the first 10 years, so the PV to the US is back-loaded and war-conditional
- US strategic priority is **of-take rights (commodity flow), not asset ownership** — a revenue model worth single-digit billions over a decade at realistic royalty volumes, not tens of billions

Honest PV estimate of the minerals/energy/reconstruction concessions to the US: **roughly $2–5 billion**, war-conditional, mostly after year 10. Compare that to the $20B deposit your structure wants up front. The concessions are worth a lot *politically* ("we got paid back") but little *financially* — which is exactly why the administration pairs them with the $300B "payback" narrative.

## 3. The structural tension your version creates

Here's the problem with scaling your full list into the exit deal. The strategic concessions can compensate **one** party:

- **If the US keeps them** (current trajectory): the third party absorbs $99B of obligations + deposit for a return of ~$122B nominal — still deeply negative NPV. The concessions make the *US* willing to exit but give the *third party* nothing. The ~$75B PV gap remains unfilled.
- **If the concessions transfer to the third party** (Europe): the US exits with only cash (deposit + annuity) and loses its "we got paid back" legacy asset — the one thing that makes exit politically sellable in Washington.
- **If Ukraine grants both** (US keeps minerals fund, Europe gets its own package plus trade/veto rights): Kyiv hits its sovereignty ceiling. The existing agreement already had to be drafted to avoid conflicting with **Ukraine's EU accession obligations** — and a European veto over reconstruction plus privileged market access would collide with EU state-aid and membership rules that Brussels will not waive.

The **veto over reconstruction** is the least feasible item on your list: 50/50 fund governance is the maximum Kyiv accepted even while militarily dependent. A hard veto requires a coercive leverage (occupation, or aid dependency far beyond today's) that contradicts the "clean exit" premise.

## 4. Welcome-ness from the US point of view

- **White House: maximal.** This is not hypothetical — it is current policy, sold as "investment, not aid," with the DFC explicitly empowered to replicate the model with other countries. The Sicomines precedent (China's $3B-for-$93B-of-minerals swap in DRC) shows resource-for-support deals are bankable — and the DRC later reneged, which is the cautionary footnote.
- **Congress: split along familiar lines** — transactional framing plays well; the "neocolonialism" critique and the appearance of monetizing an ally's distress do not.
- **Strategic community: positive**, because the real prize is supply-chain positioning against China (titanium, lithium, uranium, graphite) — not cash.

## Bottom line

Your instinct is right: **the deal is strategic concessions, not debt.** But the corrected ledger looks like this:

| Component | Value to US | Feasibility |
|---|---|---|
| 50% of new-license royalties + 50/50 fund governance | $2–5B PV, war-conditional | ✅ Already signed |
| Mineral/energy **of-take priority** | Supply-chain value, hard to monetize | ✅ Likely, via fund projects |
| Land/business **ownership** | — | ❌ Blocked (Ukrainian land law, politics) |
| Trade/import-export **privileges** | Modest; conflicts with EU accession | ⚠️ Partial only |
| **Veto** over reconstruction plan | Large symbolic value | ❌ Beyond what Kyiv/Brussels will accept |
| **Deposit + annuity** for exit | $20B + ~$4.6B/yr | Only if Europe pays as strategic subsidy |

So the endgame your model converges to is: **US keeps the minerals fund and offtake rights (legacy + "paid back" narrative), Europe pays the deposit and assumes obligations as the price of owning European security, and the annuity is backstopped by frozen Russian assets rather than Ukraine's budget.** Every element of that is already partially in motion — the plan's contribution is to price it, and the pricing says the strategic concessions justify the exit *politically* but cover only a small fraction of it *financially*.

### What “war debt” means in Ukraine’s case

Ukraine does not have a single line item called “war debt.” Instead, its **public (state + state‑guaranteed) debt** has surged since the 2022 full‑scale invasion because:

- Defence and security spending jumped to ~20% of GDP in 2022 and is budgeted at ~26% in 2025.[^1_6]

Recent macro digests and official forecasts give a consistent picture:

- **1 Jan 2026:** public + government‑guaranteed debt ≈ **USD 213.2 billion** (>100% of GDP).[^1_1]
