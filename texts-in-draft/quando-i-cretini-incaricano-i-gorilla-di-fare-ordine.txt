## x/Grok Conclusion

**Agreed.** 

Cyber threats are real and asymmetric. Hackers (state, criminal, script-kiddie) exploit exactly the poor hygiene you see in Romania/Sweden. Societies need effective response capacity. But weaponizing investigations into blanket surveillance/dossiers on innocents (or "potential threats" based on perception) is corrosive. It chills speech, creates blackmail markets, and delegitimizes institutions.

State intelligence and law enforcement agencies exist precisely for systemic and national-level threats. They are — at least in principle — embedded in a constitutional framework subject to democratic oversight, separation of powers, parliamentary control, judicial review, and periodic electoral accountability. Even when they resist or subvert that control (which they often try), it remains a contest *within* the system of public power. A change in government (Hungary-style or otherwise), a budget cut, a new majority, or a determined reformist minister can reassert direction. That's the logic of sovereign authority residing with the state, not diffused to private actors.

Your distinction between **law/norms** on one hand and the deeper **principle of Right** (diritto, Recht) on the other is important. This is classical territory: the idea that legitimacy derives not merely from formal legality or majority will, but from alignment with a public ethic grounded in observable consequences for the common good. Something is "bad" because it tends to erode trust, concentrate unaccountable power, create permanent factions with private interests in coercion, or undermine the conditions for ordered liberty and prosperity. It's consequentialist in a republican sense: what preserves the res publica over time.

### Realistic view

Human systems are indeed never perfect, nor near-perfect. Public agencies suffer from inertia, mission creep, politicization, and the classic "who watches the watchers" problem. Private actors introduce a different pathology: profit-seeking logic applied to the monopoly on legitimate violence and investigation. Both can deviate. The decisive point is **where the default locus of power sits** and what mechanisms exist to correct course.

- Entrusting core functions (deep investigations, dossiers, access to state databases) primarily to **public institutions** keeps the principal (the polity) in a position to eventually correct abuses through political means.
- Outsourcing to corporate structures fragments accountability and creates entities whose natural incentive is to expand their own influence and revenue, with easier exit options (dissolution, jurisdiction shopping).

Your earlier proposal for **temporary individual professional experts** under strict public direction fits cleanly here: it augments capacity without ceding institutional power or creating parallel power centers.

### The ethical core

You put it well: the issue isn't chasing perfection but recognizing patterns that reliably produce **net loss for everyone** in the long run. Systematic private dossier mills, even when initially "efficient," tend to:
- Weaken the state's monopoly on legitimate coercion.
- Generate conflicts of interest (security sold as a recurring service).
- Erode public trust when abuses surface.
- Create insider networks that are harder for democratic politics to dislodge than flawed but visible public agencies.

This doesn't require romanticizing the state. It requires insisting that instruments of public force and investigation remain *public* in character, with all the messy democratic correctives (however imperfect) that entails. Private competence can advise or execute narrowly defined tasks; it should not become the architecture.

This is a coherent, principled position. It prioritizes the long-term health of the political order over short-term operational convenience. In the context of the cyber incidents and doxxing concerns you shared, it explains why the instinctive reaction against delegating investigative powers to profit-driven firms feels like a defense of the "principle of the Right" rather than mere nostalgia or anti-market ideology.
